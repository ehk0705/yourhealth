Definition

Cholesterol is a fatty substance found in the blood. It is essential for building cell membranes, producing vitamin D, and making certain hormones. The body produces cholesterol (mainly in the liver), and some also comes from food.

The issue is not cholesterol itself, but excess levels, especially a high LDL level, which can promote deposits inside artery walls. Over time, these deposits may narrow arteries (atherosclerosis) and increase the risk of heart attack and stroke.

Prevention key point: most cardiovascular complications develop slowly over many years. Acting early (diet, physical activity, quitting smoking, weight management, diabetes and blood pressure control) is often far more effective than trying to catch up late.

Types of cholesterol

  • LDL: Low-Density Lipoprotein — often called “bad cholesterol”. It carries cholesterol from the liver to tissues. When too high, it promotes plaque deposits in arteries.
  • HDL: High-Density Lipoprotein — often called “good cholesterol”. It helps carry excess cholesterol back to the liver for elimination.

LDL is a major prevention target because it is directly involved in plaque formation. HDL is generally a protective marker, but it does not cancel out a very high LDL.

LDL = Low + Density + Lipoprotein HDL = High + Density + Lipoprotein

Acronyms (letter-by-letter)

  • LDL = Low – Density – Lipoprotein.
  • HDL = High – Density – Lipoprotein.

HDL / LDL ranges

Cholesterol values may be reported in mmol/L or g/L. Targets depend on overall cardiovascular risk (age, smoking, diabetes, blood pressure, family history, etc.). The higher the risk, the lower the LDL target should be.

Prevention: it helps to know your numbers (LDL, HDL, triglycerides), but also related factors (blood pressure, blood sugar, waist circumference). One number does not tell the whole story.

LDL (often called “bad cholesterol”)

  • Optimal: < 1.0 g/L (2.6 mmol/L)
  • Acceptable: 1.0 to 1.6 g/L
  • High: > 1.6 g/L

HDL (often called “good cholesterol”)

  • Low (higher risk): < 0.40 g/L in men
  • Low (higher risk): < 0.50 g/L in women
  • Protective: ≥ 0.60 g/L

In prevention, the most common goal is to lower LDL (lifestyle, and sometimes medication) and improve the overall profile (weight, activity, smoking, blood pressure, blood sugar).

Important: LDL “targets” may be lower for people with existing cardiovascular disease, diabetes, kidney disease, or multiple risk factors. Targets should be defined with a health professional.

Prevention: what matters most

Cholesterol prevention is not only about “avoiding fat”. The goal is to lower LDL sustainably and protect arteries long term. Small but consistent changes are often the most effective.

Diet (simple priorities)

  • Reduce saturated fats: processed meats, fried foods, pastries, ultra-processed products.
  • Limit trans fats (often found in some industrial foods).
  • Increase fibre: vegetables, fruits, legumes, oats, whole grains.
  • Choose better fats: olive/canola oil, nuts, seeds, fish.
  • Watch added sugar and sugary drinks (they often worsen metabolic risk and weight).

Physical activity (direct impact)

  • Realistic target: move most days (brisk walking, cycling, swimming, etc.).
  • Add light strength training twice weekly if possible.
  • Reduce sitting time: stand up and walk a few minutes regularly.

Activity improves lipids, weight, blood pressure and blood sugar: it is a major prevention lever.

Weight, waist size, metabolism

Excess abdominal fat is often linked to higher triglycerides, insulin resistance and a less favourable LDL profile. Even a modest weight loss can improve markers.

Smoking, alcohol, sleep, stress

  • Smoking: quitting is one of the best decisions for arteries.
  • Alcohol: be cautious, especially if triglycerides are high.
  • Sleep: too little sleep disrupts metabolism.
  • Chronic stress: can drive harmful habits; build simple routines (walking, breathing, regular schedule).
Prevention message: if LDL is high, doing nothing is a bad idea. High cholesterol can stay silent for years, then show up suddenly (heart attack, stroke). The right time to act is now.

Screening and follow-up

Prevention also means screening. Many people discover high LDL by chance. A lipid panel often includes LDL, HDL, total cholesterol and triglycerides.

  • Ask for screening if you have family history, diabetes, high blood pressure, excess weight, smoking, or older age.
  • Track trends over time: changes are often more informative than a single result.
  • Discuss LDL targets based on total risk, not only “normal ranges”.
Warning: do not stop or change cholesterol medication without medical advice. In secondary prevention (existing cardiovascular disease), targets are often stricter.

Causes

  • Diet high in saturated and trans fats
  • Physical inactivity
  • Overweight or obesity
  • Genetic factors
  • Smoking and alcohol
Targeted prevention: if genetics is the main driver, lifestyle still helps, but medication may be needed to reach protective LDL levels. The goal is artery protection.

This information does not replace medical advice. If you have chest pain, unusual shortness of breath, one-sided weakness, or speech problems: call emergency services.
